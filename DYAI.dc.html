DYAI Design your augmented intelligence

We called it artificial because we did not know where to put it.

Five movements on what is actually happening. Benjamin Poersch.

{{ n1 }} We called it artificial because we did not know where to put it

Nothing about it is artificial. And nothing about it is only intelligence.

Writing is artificial. Money is artificial. Contract law is artificial. We do not treat those as foreign bodies sitting outside human life. We treat them as things we made and then had to live with. Something is not outside you because you produced it.

The second word is the larger mistake. We use intelligence as if it named a property of individual people, then ask whether a machine has it. That question has consumed a decade and produced almost nothing, because it was the wrong question asked confidently.

What was built is an extension. Of language, of recall, of how much one person can hold at once. Every extension we ever made changed what we were. The alphabet did, the map did, the double entry ledger did. This one is faster and it talks back.

That is the whole reason this is called augmented and not artificial. Not branding. A correction, made early, before the word settles and stops being questionable.

■ And yet, renaming a thing is the cheapest move available. It changes what you can think, not what the thing does. “Augmented” is a comfortable word: it promises addition without loss. If it becomes a way of not asking what is being handed over, and to whom, it is worse than the word it replaces.

{{ n2 }} You were never the only intelligence in the room

A market sets prices no person could calculate. A legal system reaches conclusions no single lawyer holds. A language carries distinctions nobody designed. A research field knows things that no researcher knows.

These are not metaphors. They are the ordinary case, and the lone thinker is the exception we built a flattering story around. You have spent your whole life inside intelligences larger than you, feeding them, being shaped by them, mistaking their conclusions for your own.

What is new is not that intelligence exists outside individual heads. What is new is how quickly you can now join one, and how little the joining asks of you.

So the question was never whether machines can think. The question worth your attention is what kind of participant you are in the systems you are already inside, which you notice, which you feed, and which have started answering in your voice.

You have always been a participant in intelligence you did not own.

■ And yet, call everything intelligent and the word stops cutting anything. There is also a convenient move hidden here: if intelligence is everywhere, nobody in particular answers for what it produces. That is exactly the wrong conclusion, and it is the easy one.

“It will pass.” “It will solve everything.” Both are ways of not looking.

One declares the matter settled, the other declares it solved. Opposite conclusions, identical benefit: neither requires you to keep your eyes open. Both are finished thinking, available at no cost, and both are wrong in the same way. They answer a question that is still open.

What is harder to hold is that this is happening now, slowly, and that we are standing inside it. People will write books about this decade. Experts will argue about details that are invisible to us today, not despite our being inside, but because we are.

I do not know whether this is the largest economic shift in human history. I know we are in the middle of it, and that from the inside you see badly. Those are not modest sentences. They are the working conditions, and pretending otherwise is the only real error available right now.

■ And yet, “both extremes are wrong” is the most comfortable position in any argument. It costs nothing, it wins cheaply, and it lets me hold the middle without a number, a date or a prediction anyone could check later. Refusing to be wrong is not the same as being careful. It is just quieter.

{{ n4 }} Everyone wants the capability

Not the licence fee. The price is practised ways of working that stop making sense, areas of responsibility cut differently, and sometimes the way you understand your own competence. That last one is the expensive part and it never appears in a business case.

Which is why so many rollouts stall while the technology works perfectly. When a team does not use a tool, it is rarely the tool and almost never the people. It is the conditions both are asked to work under: who decides, who answers for the outcome, and whether anyone is permitted to be slower for a while.

This is not a training problem. Training assumes the difficulty is knowing how. The difficulty is usually knowing what happens to you if you do.

■ And yet, “letting go” is the phrase management uses to move the cost of a decision onto the people who did not make it. Sometimes resistance is not fear of change; it is an accurate reading of who carries the risk.

Almost nobody wants to pay the price. The price is letting go.

{{ n5 }} Going on was always the plan

It meant: let us see what happens if we go on. That is the through line of every period we later call formative, not confidence about the outcome, which nobody had, but a willingness to move without it.

This is not an argument for speed. Going on and rushing are different things: the first requires attention, the second is a way of avoiding it. Nor is it a claim that the outcome will be good. It is an observation that standing still was never actually on the table, and that pretending otherwise costs more than admitting it.

What is being asked of you is smaller and harder than a position on the future. Stay curious enough to keep looking. Stay sober enough to say what you do not know.

Humanity never meant that things stay as they are.

■ And yet, “we always went on” has justified a great deal of damage, usually decided by people who were not the ones absorbing it. Progress is not a law of nature. It is a series of choices, most of them made by whoever was in the room.

Let us see what happens if we go on.
